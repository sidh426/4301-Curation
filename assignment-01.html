Component 1

Topic Statement

My collection curates resources on Project Management Information Systems (PMIS) in healthcare IT: the software platforms, governance structures, and methodologies healthcare organizations use to plan, execute, and report on IT projects such as EHR rollouts, system integrations, and infrastructure upgrades. I work in this space professionally, and I'm building this collection to have one reliable place to point to for grounded, practitioner-tested guidance instead of re-researching the same ground every time a new implementation starts.

Scope: I'm including sources that address PMIS specifically within a healthcare or health IT context, including foundational methodology, healthcare PMO governance models, practitioner and academic research on PMIS effectiveness, real inspectable PMIS tools, and current industry coverage. I'm excluding general enterprise project management content not tied to IT systems (construction, manufacturing, etc.), vendor sales pages without substantive detail, and sources that only mention PMIS in passing rather than treating it as a central focus.

Component 2

Proposed Organizational Structure

I'm organizing sources by function within the PMIS knowledge domain, meaning what job a reader is trying to do when they open the source, rather than by format or publication date. Five categories:

Foundations & Methodology

Core texts establishing PMBOK-based frameworks for health IT project management.

Healthcare IT Governance & PMO Structures

How organizations build and staff the office around the system.

Research & Evidence

Practitioner surveys and academic studies on what PMIS actually delivers.

Tools & Systems

Real, inspectable PMIS software rather than secondhand descriptions.

Industry Trends & Current Practice

How PMIS is being discussed and used right now.

Rationale: My audience is healthcare IT project managers and PMP-track professionals who arrive with different needs. Someone new to the field wants Foundations, someone building a PMO wants Governance, someone justifying a tool purchase wants Research & Evidence, someone technical wants to see inside a real Tool, and everyone benefits from staying current with Industry Trends. Organizing by function rather than chronology or source type lets a reader jump straight to the category that matches the decision they're actually trying to make.

Initial Sources

Building on Exercise 01: Source Discovery (10 sources found across six discovery methods), these are the 5–7 strongest sources, expanded into full annotations and placed within the structure above.

Foundations & Methodology

The Project Manager's Guide to Health Information Technology Implementation

Susan M. Houston · HIMSS Book Series (Routledge / Productivity Press)

Category: Foundations & Methodology

This book maps PMI's PMBOK process groups and knowledge areas directly onto health IT software implementations, covering initiation through closeout for both custom-built and commercial off-the-shelf systems. Houston, a PMP and clinical informaticist, writes for practitioners rather than theorists, which is why this reads close to a reference manual for the work I actually do. Health IT project managers and PMP candidates moving into healthcare will benefit most. One limitation: it assumes familiarity with standard PMBOK terminology, so readers without a project management background should pair it with an introductory PM resource first.

93 words

Project Management for Healthcare Information Technology

Scott Coplan & David Masuda · McGraw-Hill (2011)

Category: Foundations & Methodology

Co-authored by a PMP and a practicing physician, this book synthesizes three disciplines (project management, IT/product management, and organizational change management) into one methodology for healthcare technology rollouts. What makes it useful here is that it addresses the clinical adoption and workflow-disruption problems that pure IT project management texts overlook, often the actual reason healthcare implementations fail. Project managers and IT leaders who work directly with clinical staff during system rollouts will get the most from it. Note that it was published in 2011, so specific tool references are dated, though the underlying three-discipline methodology still holds up.

Healthcare IT Governance & PMO Structures

Project Management Office in Healthcare Information Technology

L. Aziz · PMI Global Congress, via PMI.org

Category: Healthcare IT Governance & PMO Structures

This paper examines how healthcare IT PMOs differ from PMOs in other industries, outlining the governance, staffing, and tooling foundations a PMO needs to succeed inside a hospital or health system. I included it because it covers the exact organizational angle this collection focuses on: not just PMIS software, but the office and governance structure built around it. Healthcare IT leaders designing or restructuring a PMO will find it most useful. It's a conference paper rather than a peer-reviewed study, though, so its claims rely more on practitioner experience than controlled empirical data.

Research & Evidence

Project Management Information Systems Overviews

M. A. B. Ilyas, M. K. Hassan & M. U. Ilyas · PMI Global Congress 2013, EMEA, via PMI.org

Category: Research & Evidence > Practitioner Data

Based on a survey of 100 project managers across the EMEA region, this paper ranks the most-used PMIS features, the most common shortcomings, and practitioners' top wish-list items for PMIS software. It's included because it reports what real project managers experience rather than vendor claims or purely theoretical overviews, giving grounded evidence for evaluating PMIS tools. Anyone selecting or advocating for PMIS software within their organization will find it useful. The survey is now over a decade old and not healthcare-specific, so findings should be cross-checked against more recent, sector-specific sources.

91 words

Project Management Information Systems (PMIS) as a Tool for Integrated Cost and Technical Oversight

ResearchGate (2024)

Category: Research & Evidence > Academic Research

This 2024 paper examines how PMIS platforms support real-time KPI tracking and integrated cost and technical oversight on complex projects. Its main strength is currency: it offers a recent counterweight to the older practitioner surveys in this collection and connects directly to the reporting and oversight functions of PMIS that come up constantly in day-to-day healthcare IT work. Project managers and analysts focused on the reporting and dashboard side of PMIS rather than implementation will get the most from it. It isn't healthcare-specific, though, so its examples require some translation to a hospital or health-system context.

96 words

Tools & Systems

OpenProject (opf/openproject)

GitHub repository, open-source PMIS

Category: Tools & Systems

OpenProject is an open-source PMIS whose full codebase, issue tracker, and architecture documentation are publicly available on GitHub. That makes it possible to see how a real PMIS is actually built, including its data models, permissions, and reporting modules, instead of relying only on vendor descriptions or marketing pages. Project managers or IT staff evaluating whether to self-host a PMIS, or anyone wanting to understand PMIS architecture hands-on, will find it most useful. Limitation: OpenProject is a general-purpose PMIS, not built for healthcare specifically, so it won't reflect healthcare compliance or clinical workflow features out of the box.

98 words

Industry Trends & Current Practice

A Complete Guide on Project Management Information Systems

TechRepublic

Category: Industry Trends & Current Practice

This guide walks through PMIS across the full project lifecycle, pulling implementation examples from several industries and covering best practices like setting clear objectives before configuring a tool. It works well as a current, practitioner-facing counterpoint to the older academic and book sources in this collection, showing how PMIS is being discussed in industry right now. General readers, new project managers, or anyone wanting a fast, non-academic overview will find it most accessible. It's broad and vendor-neutral rather than healthcare-focused, though, so it should be read alongside the more specialized sources here.
