Project Management Information Systems (PMIS) in Healthcare IT

Sid Bhardwaj · sidhbhardwaj426@gmail.com

This collection gathers eighteen sources on Project Management Information Systems (PMIS) in healthcare IT: the software platforms, governance structures, and methodologies healthcare organizations use to plan, execute, and report on IT projects such as EHR rollouts, system integrations, and infrastructure upgrades. It grew out of Assignment 01, a planning document that scoped the topic and proposed the organizational structure used here, and it now includes real books, peer-reviewed research, a podcast, a video, an online course, a government resource, industry news, an open-source tool, and a commercial vendor case study, deliberately spanning depth and format rather than repeating the same kind of source eighteen times.

Sources are organized into five categories, each answering a different question a reader might bring to this collection. Foundations and Methodology is the starting point for anyone new to the topic. Healthcare IT Governance and PMO Structures covers how organizations build and run the office around the system. Research and Evidence holds the peer-reviewed and survey-based sources for readers who want data rather than opinion. Tools and Systems shows real PMIS platforms, open-source and commercial, in use. Industry Trends and Current Practice covers how PMIS is being discussed and applied right now.

To use this collection, start with whichever category matches what you are trying to do rather than reading top to bottom. Each entry names its category and format, links to the original source, and includes a short annotation explaining what it covers, why it is here, who benefits most, and where its limits are.

Foundations & Methodology

Core texts, frameworks, and entry points for understanding what a PMIS is and how it fits into a health IT project.

The Project Manager's Guide to Health Information Technology Implementation

Susan M. Houston · HIMSS Book Series (Routledge / Productivity Press), 3rd ed.

Category: Foundations & Methodology Format: Book

This book maps PMI's PMBOK process groups and knowledge areas directly onto health IT software implementations, walking through initiation, planning, execution, monitoring, and closeout for both custom-built and commercial off-the-shelf clinical systems. Houston, a PMP and clinical informaticist with a nursing background, writes for practitioners rather than theorists, using real implementation scenarios instead of abstract examples, which is why this reads closer to a working reference manual than a textbook. Health IT project managers, PMP candidates moving into healthcare, and IT directors overseeing clinical system rollouts will get the most out of it. One limitation: it assumes readers already know standard PMBOK terminology, so someone with no project management background at all should pair it with an introductory PM resource before diving in. Now in its third edition, it stays current with modern implementation practice.

Project Management for Healthcare Information Technology

Scott Coplan & David Masuda · McGraw-Hill (2011)

Category: Foundations & Methodology Format: Book

Co-authored by a PMP and a practicing physician, this book synthesizes three disciplines (project management, IT/product management, and organizational change management) into one integrated methodology built specifically for healthcare technology rollouts. What makes it useful is that it treats clinical adoption and workflow disruption as core project risks rather than afterthoughts, addressing the exact problems that pure IT project management texts tend to overlook and that are often the real reason healthcare implementations fail. Project managers and IT leaders who work directly with clinical staff during EHR or system rollouts, and who need buy-in from physicians and nurses, will benefit most. Note that it was published in 2011, so specific software and tool references are dated, though the underlying three-discipline framework for balancing technical, clinical, and change management concerns still holds up well today.

Foundations of Healthcare Project Management

Coursera

Category: Foundations & Methodology Format: Online Course

This Coursera course introduces the foundational concepts of project management specifically within a healthcare context, aimed at learners who are new to formal project management altogether rather than experienced PMs looking to specialize. It covers core PM vocabulary, healthcare-specific constraints like regulatory compliance and clinical stakeholder management, and basic planning and execution steps, structured as a guided, self-paced learning path rather than a static reference. It earns a place here because it offers a structured, beginner-friendly on-ramp that the denser books and papers in this collection don't provide, and because video-based instruction suits some learners better than text. People entirely new to healthcare project management, including clinical staff moving into project coordination roles, will benefit most. As you'd expect from an introductory course, experienced project managers will find little new material, and a subscription or one-time payment is required to access it in full.

Project management information system

Wikipedia

Category: Foundations & Methodology Format: Reference

This Wikipedia article defines project management information systems in general terms, covering the core components, categories of PMIS software, and how a PMIS differs from a general project management system, without any healthcare-specific framing. It's here as a deliberately neutral, jargon-free baseline: before engaging with healthcare-specific sources elsewhere in this collection, a reader unfamiliar with the term benefits from seeing the general definition first, since several other sources here assume it. Students, career-changers, or anyone encountering the term PMIS for the first time will find it most useful as a starting point. Keep in mind that Wikipedia articles are collaboratively edited and can change or be incomplete at any time, and this one in particular offers no healthcare context at all, so it should be treated strictly as a glossary-style entry point rather than a substantive source in its own right.

Health IT Playbook

Office of the National Coordinator for Health IT (ONC) · HealthIT.gov

Category: Foundations & Methodology Format: Government Guide

The Office of the National Coordinator for Health IT's Health IT Playbook is a free, federally maintained implementation guide covering EHR selection, planning, security risk assessment, and patient engagement, built for administrators and practice staff navigating a health IT project from start to finish. What sets it apart is that it's an authoritative, vendor-neutral government resource rather than a commercial or academic one, offering practical tools like an EHR contract guide and a security risk assessment tool alongside its planning guidance. Practice administrators, project managers at smaller health systems without a formal PMO, and clinicians stepping into project coordination roles will find it most directly usable. It's oriented toward outpatient practices and smaller organizations selecting and implementing an EHR for the first time, though, so large health systems with mature PMOs running complex, multi-system programs will find it less directly applicable than the enterprise-focused sources elsewhere in this collection.

Healthcare IT Governance & PMO Structures

How organizations build, staff, and run the office around the PMIS, illustrated with real examples.

Project Management Office in Healthcare Information Technology

L. Aziz · PMI Global Congress, via PMI.org

Category: Governance & PMO Structures Format: Practitioner Paper

This PMI Global Congress paper examines how healthcare IT PMOs differ structurally from PMOs in other industries, covering the governance models, staffing patterns, and tooling decisions a PMO needs to succeed inside a hospital or health system. What earns it a place here is that it addresses the exact organizational angle this collection focuses on: not just PMIS software, but the office and governance structure built around it, including how PMO authority and reporting lines typically work in a clinical organization. Healthcare IT leaders designing or restructuring a PMO, or project managers trying to understand where their office fits in the broader organization, will find it most useful. Worth noting: it's a conference paper rather than a peer-reviewed journal study, so its claims rely more on practitioner experience and case observation than controlled empirical research, and it should be read alongside more recent, data-driven sources in this collection.

Ask the Healthcare PMO Expert: Why Your Projects Aren't Delivering (And How to Fix It)

Optimum Healthcare IT

Category: Governance & PMO Structures Format: Video

This recorded expert conversation addresses why healthcare IT projects commonly fail to deliver and what a PMO can do differently, covering issues like unclear intake processes, weak prioritization, and insufficient executive sponsorship in hospital settings. It's a useful, shorter-format entry point into PMO-specific pain points compared to the longer academic and book sources elsewhere in this collection, and its conversational format surfaces the kind of practical, on-the-ground frustration that formal papers tend to smooth over. Healthcare IT project managers and PMO leaders who want a quick, relatable diagnostic of common PMO dysfunction, rather than a full framework, will benefit most. Worth flagging: it's produced by a healthcare IT consulting firm, so it has a promotional undertone and should be treated as an expert opinion piece rather than independent research; it's most useful for generating discussion points rather than citable data.

UI Health Care Project Management Office

University of Iowa Health Care

Category: Governance & PMO Structures Format: Organization Page

The University of Iowa Health Care Project Management Office publishes its own standards, status-reporting templates, tools, and consultation services directly on this page, making it a real, currently operating example of how one academic health system structures and documents its PMO rather than a secondhand description of one. What makes it worth including is that it shows the actual artifacts a functioning healthcare PMO produces, like standardized status reports and project templates, giving something concrete to compare against a reader's own workplace PMO or the theoretical PMO models discussed elsewhere in this collection. Healthcare IT PMO leaders setting up or refining their own office's documentation and reporting standards will find it most directly useful. It reflects one specific academic medical center's approach and scale, though, so smaller community hospitals or larger multi-facility systems may need to adapt its templates and processes rather than adopt them directly.

Research & Evidence

Practitioner surveys, case data, and peer-reviewed studies on what actually drives PMIS and healthcare IT project outcomes.

Project Management Information Systems Overviews

M. A. B. Ilyas, M. K. Hassan & M. U. Ilyas · PMI Global Congress 2013, EMEA, via PMI.org

Category: Research & Evidence Format: Practitioner Survey

Based on a survey of 100 project managers across the EMEA region, this paper ranks the most-used PMIS features, the most common software shortcomings, and practitioners' top wish-list items, giving a concrete, feature-level picture of what project managers actually want from PMIS tools rather than what vendors claim their tools do. The reason it's here is that it reports lived practitioner experience, which is more useful for evaluating real PMIS options than a purely theoretical overview would be. Anyone selecting, configuring, or advocating for PMIS software within their organization, especially IT directors comparing vendors, will find it useful for building an evidence-based case. The survey is now over a decade old and not healthcare-specific, so its findings should be cross-checked against more recent, sector-specific sources elsewhere in this collection, particularly the 2024 paper on PMIS and cost oversight.

Project Management Information Systems (PMIS) as a Tool for Integrated Cost and Technical Oversight

ResearchGate (2024)

Category: Research & Evidence Format: Academic Paper

This 2024 paper examines how PMIS platforms support real-time KPI tracking and integrated cost and technical oversight on complex projects, focusing specifically on how dashboards and reporting modules let managers monitor budget, schedule, and technical risk from one system. Its main strength is currency: it offers a recent counterweight to the older practitioner surveys in this collection and connects directly to the reporting and oversight functions of PMIS that come up constantly in day-to-day healthcare IT work, where budget variance and technical risk both need visibility. Project managers and analysts focused on the reporting and dashboard side of PMIS, rather than initial implementation or vendor selection, will get the most from it. It isn't healthcare-specific, though, so its cost-oversight examples require some translation to a hospital or health-system budgeting context before they're directly useful.

Electronic Health Records (EHR) Deployment Projects: Best/Worst Practices

PMI Global Congress, via PMI.org

Category: Research & Evidence Format: Practitioner Paper

Based on PMI's own library, this paper examines electronic health record deployment projects specifically through the lens of documented best and worst practices, drawing on real project post-mortems to identify what separated successful EHR rollouts from failed ones. This one earns its spot because EHR deployment is one of the largest and highest-risk project types a healthcare IT PMO handles, and this source addresses that specific project type directly rather than healthcare IT project management in general. Project managers actively planning or running an EHR deployment, and PMO leaders building intake checklists or risk frameworks for similar projects, will find it most directly applicable. As a PMI Global Congress paper rather than a peer-reviewed journal article, its findings are drawn from practitioner case reporting rather than a controlled study, so it works best alongside the peer-reviewed research elsewhere in this collection.

The Impact of Leadership and Management on the Implementation of Electronic Health Record Systems in the Primary Healthcare Centers

PMC (PubMed Central), peer-reviewed

Category: Research & Evidence Format: Journal Article

This peer-reviewed journal article examines how leadership and management practices affect the success of electronic health record implementations in primary healthcare centers, using empirical data rather than practitioner anecdote to identify which management behaviors correlate with successful adoption. Its main contribution is being the collection's clearest peer-reviewed, empirically grounded source, giving the annotations here a real research backbone rather than relying only on practitioner reports and vendor content. Researchers, students, and healthcare IT leaders who want evidence-based justification for leadership and governance decisions, rather than just practitioner opinion, will find it most useful. The catch: it focuses on primary care centers specifically, which are smaller and structurally different from the hospital or health-system PMOs most other sources in this collection address, so its findings should be scaled carefully rather than applied directly to large enterprise implementations.

Tools & Systems

Real PMIS platforms and assessment frameworks, open-source and commercial, in actual use.

OpenProject (opf/openproject)

GitHub repository, open-source PMIS

Category: Tools & Systems Format: Open-Source Tool

OpenProject is an open-source PMIS whose full codebase, issue tracker, and architecture documentation are publicly available on GitHub, covering everything from its underlying data models to its permission structure and reporting modules. That makes it possible to see exactly how a real PMIS is built and configured, instead of relying only on vendor descriptions or marketing pages, which is rare for a working project management platform. Project managers or IT staff evaluating whether to self-host a PMIS, developers considering contributing to or extending an open-source tool, and anyone wanting to understand PMIS architecture hands-on will find it most useful. The catch is that OpenProject is a general-purpose PMIS, not built for healthcare specifically, so it won't reflect healthcare compliance requirements or clinical workflow features out of the box, and evaluating it seriously requires enough technical comfort to install and configure it.

Electronic Medical Record Adoption Model (EMRAM)

HIMSS

Category: Tools & Systems Format: Maturity Model

HIMSS's Electronic Medical Record Adoption Model (EMRAM) is a maturity assessment framework, not a PMIS itself, but it defines the stages and outcome benchmarks that healthcare IT projects are frequently measured against, making it essential context for anyone reporting project status upward in a health system. Recently updated to take a more outcomes-driven approach covering clinical outcomes, patient safety, and patient experience, it's widely used internationally as a benchmark for EHR maturity. It matters here because PMIS reporting in healthcare IT often needs to map project milestones to EMRAM stages for executive and board audiences, so understanding the model itself is a prerequisite. Healthcare IT project managers, CIOs, and analysts building status reports or business cases tied to EMR maturity will find it most useful. Keep in mind it measures organizational maturity, not project execution, so it's a complement to a PMIS rather than a replacement for one.

Parkview Health: Optimizing Project Investments with PPM

Planview, vendor case study

Category: Tools & Systems Format: Vendor Case Study

This case study describes how Parkview Health, a nonprofit health system, implemented Planview's portfolio management software to replace an informal process where nearly every project request was approved regardless of business justification, strategic alignment, or available funding and staff capacity. Its value here is as a real example of a commercial, enterprise-grade PMIS deployment in a healthcare setting, complementing the open-source OpenProject example elsewhere in this collection with a look at how a paid, vendor-supported platform gets adopted and what problem it was bought to solve. IT leaders evaluating commercial PMIS options, or PMO leaders trying to build the business case for formal project intake, will find it most useful. It's published by the vendor itself, though, so it emphasizes success and should be read as a marketing case study rather than an independent evaluation, best balanced against the vendor-neutral sources elsewhere in this collection.

Industry Trends & Current Practice

How PMIS in healthcare IT is being discussed and applied right now, in practitioners' own words and in current reporting.

A Complete Guide on Project Management Information Systems

TechRepublic

Category: Industry Trends & Current Practice Format: Article

This guide walks through PMIS across the full project lifecycle, from initiation to closeout, pulling implementation examples from several industries and covering best practices like setting clear objectives and success criteria before configuring a tool. It serves as a current, practitioner-facing counterpoint to the older academic and book sources in this collection, showing how PMIS is being discussed in industry right now rather than in a 2011 or 2013 publication. General readers, new project managers, or anyone wanting a fast, non-academic overview before going deeper into the more specialized sources here will find it most accessible. It's broad and vendor-neutral rather than healthcare-focused, so its examples skew toward generic corporate IT projects, and it should be read alongside the healthcare-specific sources in this collection rather than as a standalone healthcare resource.

Healthcare Innovation: Keeping the Focus on Patients

Projectified Podcast, PMI

Category: Industry Trends & Current Practice Format: Podcast

In this Projectified episode, the director of the enterprise project management team at Moffitt Cancer Center (winner of PMI's 2021 PMO of the Year award) discusses how the organization's PMO approached innovation and adapted its project intake and delivery during the pandemic. Its value lies in being a first-person account from a healthcare PMO leader at an award-recognized organization, offering the kind of candid, day-to-day perspective that formal papers and books rarely capture, including how priorities shifted under real operational pressure. Healthcare IT PMO leaders and project managers wanting to hear how a top-performing healthcare PMO actually operates, in the PMO leader's own words, will benefit most. It's a single organization's experience and a conversational format rather than a rigorous study, though, so its value is illustrative and motivational rather than a generalizable framework, and it should be paired with more structured sources for implementation detail.

The Year in IT at 10 Major Health Systems

Becker's Hospital Review

Category: Industry Trends & Current Practice Format: News Article

Becker's Hospital Review's year-end roundup profiles the most significant health IT projects across ten major health systems, covering large-scale initiatives such as Adventist Health's Epic rollout across 27 hospitals and Northwell Health's enterprise EHR implementation, with details on scale, timeline, and organizational impact. Its usefulness is in being a snapshot of what large-scale healthcare IT project management actually looks like in practice right now, at a scale most other sources in this collection only discuss theoretically, showing real project scope and duration. Healthcare IT executives, project managers benchmarking their own project scope against peer organizations, and students researching real-world case examples will find it most useful. As a news roundup rather than an in-depth case study, though, each health system's story gets only a few paragraphs, so readers wanting deeper detail on any one project will need to follow up with that health system's own reporting.
